# Index of musical terms

Every musical word used in this documentation, in alphabetical order, explained in a sentence.

Notation has a vocabulary, most of it Italian, and almost none of it is
guessable. This is the back of the book: if a chapter used a word and assumed
you knew it, it is here. Where Gscore does something with a term, the entry
says where.

<dl class="terms">

<div id="a"><dt>Accidental</dt><dd>A sharp, flat or natural written in front of
a note, changing it for the rest of that bar. <span class="seen">Keys
<b>+</b>, <b>−</b>, <b>#</b>, <b>_</b>, <b>=</b>.</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Accent</dt><dd>A wedge over a note meaning strike it harder than its
neighbours. Gscore plays it, not just draws it. <span class="seen">Key
<b>!</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Anacrusis</dt><dd>Also called a pickup or upbeat: the incomplete bar a
piece begins on, before the first strong beat. Bar <i>one</i> is the first
<i>full</i> bar, which is why the numbering looks offset until you know.
<span class="seen">Score → Pickup measure…</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Articulation</dt><dd>How a note is attacked and released — staccato,
tenuto, accent, marcato — as against how loud it is or how long it is
written.</dd></div>

<div id="b"><dt>Bar</dt><dd>See <i>measure</i>. The two words mean the same
thing; British players say bar, American scores say measure, and Gscore
answers to both.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Barline</dt><dd>The vertical line closing a bar. Single, double (a
section ends), final (the piece ends), or a repeat sign.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Beam</dt><dd>The thick line joining the stems of short notes
together, instead of each one carrying its own flag. How notes are grouped
under beams tells you where the beats are, which is why Gscore groups them by
the meter rather than by how many will fit. <span class="seen">Key
<b>b</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Bend</dt><dd>On a guitar, pushing a string sideways to raise the
pitch after it has been struck. <span class="seen">Insert →
Tab</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Brace</dt><dd>The curly bracket joining the two staves of one
instrument, a piano most often. Distinct from a <i>bracket</i>, which joins a
family of different instruments.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Buzz roll</dt><dd>A snare drum roll whose strokes are not counted —
pressed rather than struck. Written as a tremolo with a Z through the stem.
<span class="seen">Insert → Tremolo</span></dd></div>

<div id="c"><dt>Chord</dt><dd>Two or more notes sounding at once, written as
noteheads sharing a stem. <span class="seen"><b>Mod</b>+<b>Space</b>, or
<code>[ceg]</code> when typing</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Chord symbol</dt><dd>The name of a chord written above the staff —
<code>C7#9</code>, <code>Fm7♭5</code> — for a player who will voice it
themselves. <span class="seen">Key <b>c</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Clef</dt><dd>The sign at the head of a staff saying which lines are
which notes. Treble, bass, alto, tenor, and percussion, which names
instruments rather than pitches.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Compound metre</dt><dd>A meter whose beats divide into three rather
than two: 6/8 is <i>two</i> beats of a dotted quarter, not six of an eighth.
Gscore beams it in threes and its metronome clicks twice a bar, because that
is what anybody counting it is counting.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Concert pitch</dt><dd>What a note actually sounds, as against what
its player reads. A clarinet in B flat reads a C and a B flat comes out.
<span class="seen">View → Concert pitch</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Crescendo</dt><dd>Getting louder, drawn as an opening wedge.
<span class="seen">Key <b>&lt;</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Cross-staff</dt><dd>A note belonging to one hand's line but written
on the other hand's staff, so a piano passage crossing the middle stays
readable. <span class="seen">Edit → Cross staff</span></dd></div>

<div id="d"><dt>Diminuendo</dt><dd>Getting quieter, drawn as a closing wedge.
Also called <i>decrescendo</i>. <span class="seen">Key
<b>&gt;</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Dot</dt><dd>A dot after a notehead adds half its length again; two
dots add half of that again. Not to be confused with a staccato dot, which
sits above or below the note and means something else
entirely.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Dynamic</dt><dd>How loud, written as an Italian abbreviation from
<i>pppp</i> to <i>fff</i>. It holds until another replaces it, and Gscore
plays what it says.</dd></div>

<div id="e"><dt>Engraving</dt><dd>The craft of setting music on a page so it
can be read at speed — spacing, stem direction, where marks sit, what clears
what. <span class="seen">Score → Engraving…</span></dd></div>

<div id="f"><dt>Fingering</dt><dd>Which fingers, or which keys, produce a
note. For flute there is rarely only one answer, which is why Gscore carries
every fingering the instrument has rather than one chart.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Flat</dt><dd>Lowers a note by a semitone (♭). A double flat (𝄫) lowers
it by two.</dd></div>

<div id="g"><dt>Grand staff</dt><dd>Two staves braced together, treble over
bass, read by one player — the piano's usual home.</dd></div>

<div><dt>GUIDO</dt><dd>A text format for music notation, readable and writable
by Gscore.</dd></div>

<div id="h"><dt>Hairpin</dt><dd>The wedge drawn for a crescendo or diminuendo,
so called because that is what it looks like.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Hammer-on</dt><dd>On a guitar, sounding a note by slapping a finger
onto the string rather than picking it. Its opposite is a
<i>pull-off</i>.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Harmonic</dt><dd>A bell-like tone got by touching a string lightly
instead of stopping it. Natural, artificial and pinch harmonics are three ways
of producing one.</dd></div>

<div id="i"><dt>Interval</dt><dd>The distance between two pitches, counted
inclusively: C up to E is a third, because you count C, D, E.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Isorhythm</dt><dd>A rhythmic pattern repeated over and over while the
pitches change against it — the sort of thing worth writing a script to
generate rather than typing out.</dd></div>

<div id="k"><dt>Key signature</dt><dd>The sharps or flats at the head of every
staff, saying which notes are altered throughout. Gscore's <b>Keys and
modulations</b> window reads the key from the notes instead, which is how it
catches a passage that has moved without anybody changing the
signature.</dd></div>

<div id="l"><dt>Ledger line</dt><dd>The short line drawn above or below a
staff for a note too high or too low to sit on it.</dd></div>

<div><dt>LilyPond</dt><dd>A music typesetting program with a text input
language, used where the finest possible printed result matters. Gscore
exports to it.</dd></div>

<div id="m"><dt>Marcato</dt><dd>Marked, stressed — a stronger accent, drawn as
a caret. <span class="seen">Key <b>^</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Measure</dt><dd>One bar's worth of music, between two barlines, as
long as the time signature says.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Metronome mark</dt><dd>How fast, given as a note value and a number a
minute: ♩ = 120. <span class="seen">View → Tempo marking draws it; View →
Metronome marking draws the beats themselves</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Modulation</dt><dd>A piece changing key part-way through, with or
without a new key signature to announce it.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Mordent</dt><dd>An ornament: the written note, the note next to it,
and the written note again, played quickly. The word is in the quick start
only as an example of a word you would not guess.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Multi-measure rest</dt><dd>A run of silent bars drawn as one bar with
the count over it, so a player counting fourteen bars' rest has something to
count. <span class="seen">View → Multi-measure rests</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>MusicXML</dt><dd>The standard interchange format between notation
programs. Gscore reads and writes it, compressed (<code>.mxl</code>) or
not.</dd></div>

<div id="n"><dt>Natural</dt><dd>Cancels a sharp or flat (♮), returning a note
to its plain self for the rest of the bar.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Notehead</dt><dd>The blob itself, without its stem, flag or beam.
What Gscore colours when you colour notes by name.</dd></div>

<div id="o"><dt>Octave</dt><dd>The distance from a note to the next note of
the same name, up or down. Gscore counts them <i>scientifically</i>: middle C
is <code>c4</code>.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Octave line</dt><dd>A dashed line marked 8va, 8vb, 15ma or 15mb,
meaning play this passage an octave or two away from where it is written —
which keeps a high passage off a thicket of ledger lines.</dd></div>

<div id="p"><dt>Palm mute</dt><dd>Damping the strings with the side of the
picking hand for a tight, short sound. Written <i>P.M.</i></dd></div>

<div><dt>Part</dt><dd>One player's own copy, carrying only their staff. A
score is what the conductor reads; the parts are what the stands
hold.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Pickup</dt><dd>See <i>anacrusis</i>.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Pitch</dt><dd>Which note, properly: a step (the letter), an octave,
and an alteration. Keeping those three apart — rather than storing a position
on a staff — is what lets transposition keep your spelling.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Pull-off</dt><dd>The opposite of a hammer-on: lifting a finger to
sound the note underneath.</dd></div>

<div id="r"><dt>Range</dt><dd>The span from the lowest to the highest note an
instrument can play. Gscore's <b>Range</b> window compares what you wrote
against what the instrument has.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Rehearsal mark</dt><dd>A boxed letter or number in the score so a
conductor can say "from letter M" and everyone finds it. Gscore stores it as a
place in a sequence, so inserting one renumbers the rest.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Repeat</dt><dd>A sign sending the player back to do a passage again.
A <i>first-time</i> and <i>second-time</i> ending (a <i>volta</i>) says what
differs between the two passes.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Rest</dt><dd>A measured silence. As much a part of the rhythm as a
note, and written with the same care. <span class="seen">Key
<b>Enter</b></span></dd></div>

<div id="s"><dt>Score</dt><dd>Every part written one above another, so the
whole piece can be read at once.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Semitone</dt><dd>The smallest step in ordinary Western music: one
piano key to the next, black or white.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Sharp</dt><dd>Raises a note by a semitone (♯). A double sharp (𝄪)
raises it by two.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Slur</dt><dd>A curve over a group of notes meaning play them as one
phrase, without re-articulating each. Looks like a tie and means something
quite different. <span class="seen">Key <b>S</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>SMuFL</dt><dd>Standard Music Font Layout — the agreement about which
musical symbol lives at which character in a font. Gscore ships Bravura, which
follows it.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Staccato</dt><dd>Detached: a dot above or below the note, shortening
it without making it quieter. <i>Staccatissimo</i> is shorter still.
<span class="seen">Keys <b>,</b> and <b>'</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Staff</dt><dd>The five lines music is written on. Plural
<i>staves</i>.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Staff space</dt><dd>The gap between two staff lines, and the unit
everything in engraving is measured in — so a page looks right at any size,
on any paper, on any screen.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Stem</dt><dd>The vertical line on a notehead. Which way it points is
decided by where the note sits, and is overridable when the music needs it.
<span class="seen">Keys <b>u</b> and <b>d</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Strip</dt><dd>Gscore's own word for the continuous, unpaginated view:
one unbroken line of music, which is the shape writing wants. The 2001 program
had no other.</dd></div>

<div><dt>System</dt><dd>One line of music across the page, with every staff
that is playing in it, joined at the left.</dd></div>

<div id="t"><dt>Tablature</dt><dd>Guitar notation written as string and fret
rather than as pitch: six lines, a number on each saying where the finger
goes. <span class="seen">Staff → Write as tablature…</span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Tempo</dt><dd>How fast, as a word (<i>Andante</i>), a metronome mark
(♩ = 58), or both.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Tenuto</dt><dd>Held: a short line over the note meaning give it its
full length. <span class="seen">Key <b>:</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Tie</dt><dd>A curve joining two noteheads of the <i>same</i> pitch,
making them one longer sound. Compare a <i>slur</i>, which joins different
notes. <span class="seen">Key <b>T</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Time signature</dt><dd>Two numbers at the head of the music: how many
beats in a bar, and what kind of note gets one.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Transposition</dt><dd>Moving music up or down by a fixed interval,
keeping its spelling sensible. Also what a transposing instrument does by
nature — see <i>concert pitch</i>.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Tremolo</dt><dd>A rapid repetition: slashes through a stem for one
note, between two stems for a pair alternating.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Trill</dt><dd>Rapid alternation between a note and the one above it.
A pair of notes rather than a note, which is why fingering charts list trills
separately.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Tuplet</dt><dd>A group of notes played in the time of a different
number of them — a triplet is three in the time of two. The figure over the
group says which. <span class="seen"><b>Mod</b>+<b>2</b> to
<b>9</b></span></dd></div>

<div><dt>Twelve-tone row</dt><dd>An ordering of all twelve pitches used as the
basis of a piece. The sort of thing the script console exists
for.</dd></div>

<div id="v"><dt>Velocity</dt><dd>How hard a note is struck, in MIDI terms —
what a dynamic and an accent turn into when the music is played.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Voice</dt><dd>One independent line of music. Two voices on one staff
are written <i>across</i> each other, not after each other, which is how a
held bass note sits under a moving part.</dd></div>

<div><dt>Volta</dt><dd>The bracket over a first- or second-time ending, marked
1. or 2.</dd></div>

</dl>
